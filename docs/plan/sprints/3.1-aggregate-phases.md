## Decisions to make

| Decision | Status |
| -------- | ------ |
| What the continuous score measures | **Decided.** Total **mana value of spells cast** — printed cost, not mana spent |

### D1 — Aggregate means the cards are real and the turn is not

§3's claim is that "state is dominated by totals rather than order" from turn 5.
The aggregation is that sentence made executable, and nothing more:

- **The cards are real.** The phase draws exactly what the shuffle dealt. That is
  what keeps it a sample from the same distribution as the exact phase and keeps
  §7.8's common random numbers intact — two policies compared at game *g* see the
  same cards in both phases.
- **The turn is not.** The whole phase is *one mana budget* and *one pool of
  castable cards*. A land drawn on the last turn of the phase is available to the
  whole phase's budget. That over-counts, and the over-count **is** the claim
  under test: it is exactly the sequencing §3 says stops mattering.

The budget is a closed form rather than a turn loop, which is what keeps this
from being "a turn loop with the turns hidden":

```
  D = min(T, lands available)                   land drops over the phase
  M = T·(L₀ + P) + D(D+1)/2 + (T − D)·D         mana over the whole phase
  peak = L₀ + P + D                             mana on the phase's last turn
```

**Colour and quantity are decoupled, and that decoupling is the aggregation.**
The exact phase couples them per turn: this turn's pool must pay this turn's
pips. The aggregate phase asks whether the *mana base* can produce the pips at
all (Hall's condition against the peak pool, no accumulated demand) and
separately whether the *budget* covers the total cost. Stating it this way makes
the approximation legible instead of buried.

**Ramp deployed in a phase pays into the next phase, never its own.** One rule,
and the conservative direction. Modelling a rock that pays the turn after it
lands would put order back in.

### D2 — The score is mana value deployed

> **The score is the total mana value of every spell cast, commander included.**

Chosen against the alternatives for reasons that are checkable:

- **Not mana *spent*.** A cost reducer is an efficiency the deck earned; scoring
  the discounted number would penalise it. A 6-drop cast for 4 deployed 6.
- **Lands score zero, and that is load-bearing.** §13.3 requires the 60-land deck
  to rank badly. If board presence scored, it would rank *well* — it makes every
  land drop. The known-bad fixtures are the discipline that settles this, and
  they settle it before the score exists rather than after it embarrasses one.
- **Not a weighted sum of the state vector.** That is the failure this section
  was written to prevent: seven fields with seven coefficients is not a
  measurement, it is a place to hide a fit.

**What it measures is development, not power.** The model cannot see card
quality; mana value is the game's own proxy for it, and the substitution is a
guess — §4 says so plainly and §13.4 is where it gets checked. Recorded here so
the guess is visible rather than implied.

### D3 — The opening vector is not enough, and the sprint proves it

The 13 bytes say how much mana there is and **not what is left to spend it on**.
The library cursor, the hand's contents and the board's identities are all
absent, because the vector was built to be *scored* and this needs it to be
*resumed from*.

Rather than assert that, T1 exhibits **two games with byte-identical state
vectors and different futures**. If that test cannot be written the decision is
wrong and the vector is sufficient — which is the point of writing it.

The handoff is therefore the live state (`mf_live`: board, hand, library
cursor), and `mf_phase_state` **does not grow**. Widening the scored vector to
carry resume state would have conflated two jobs and moved every golden file.

## Risks carried in

- **A gate threshold in standard errors leaves the sample size blank** (2.3). Significance is free at
  scale. Anything graded here needs an effect size beside it, and so does G4 in 3.3.
- **The skill gap is ~88% mulligan, and that component is the same on every deck** (2.3). A scalar
  gap is the wrong summary; report the components when G3 is re-measured.
- **G1 counted a clause as modelled when the opcode exists, but the metacard cannot carry the amount
  the opcode needs** (2.2) — draw and cost reduction both assume one. Widening the phases re-opens
  G1 and this is part of what it will find.
- **Inert is relative to what is simulated** (1.2.1). 90.3% of clauses are inert for turns 1–4; that
  falls here, and G1 falls with it. That is the model growing rather than breaking, and the inert
  share is quoted beside the fraction, always.
- **A defect can hide from a state vector** (2.2). The opening's reports the pool *after* everything
  untaps, so within-turn timing is invisible through it. Whatever this sprint's vector omits will
  hide the same way.
